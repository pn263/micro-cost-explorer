The production function

In the short run your factory has one cutting table and a fixed line of sewing machines: capital is fixed. The only thing you can change by tomorrow is how many workers you hire. So the question is simple: what happens to output as you add workers?

The sewing line

Key idea

A production function shows how much output a firm gets from its inputs: Q = f(K, L).

In the short run, capital is fixed at K̅, so Q = f(K̅, L) depends only on the number of workers L.

Part 2 of 7

Marginal and average product

How much does one more worker add? That number is the marginal product. It is the slope of the production function from Part 1.

MPL = ΔQ / ΔLAPL = Q / L

Diminishing marginal product

  • The 2nd worker helps a lot: one cuts, one sews (specialization, as on an assembly line).
  • From the 3rd worker on, everyone shares one cutting table and the same few machines. Workers wait for a free machine, so each new worker adds fewer T-shirts.
  • That is why the production function gets flatter as L grows.

Part 3 of 7

From production to total cost

Now put prices on the inputs. Rent and the machine lease are paid no matter how many T-shirts you sew. Each worker is paid by the hour. How does the production function turn into a total cost curve?

VC = w × LTC = FC + VC

Key idea

The TC curve gets steeper because the production function gets flatter. Every worker costs the same wage, but with diminishing MP each extra T-shirt needs more labor time, so it costs more.

The cost table and the cost curves

To talk about the cost of one unit, we count output in dozens of T-shirts per hour. You are given output Q and total cost TC. Fill in the rest, then build the four cost curves one at a time.

ATC answers: how much does a dozen cost on average?

Part 5 of 7

Why does MC cut ATC at its lowest point?

Look at Part 4 again: the MC curve crosses AVC and ATC exactly at their lowest points. This is not a coincidence. Think of your GPA.

Your GPA

  • Your next course grade is the marginal grade.
  • Below your average ⇒ your GPA falls. Above your average ⇒ your GPA rises.

Same for costs

  • MC < ATC ⇒ ATC falls.
  • MC > ATC ⇒ ATC rises.
  • So MC crosses ATC where ATC stops falling and starts rising: its lowest point. The same holds for AVC.

What to look for

Part 7 of 7

Recap: one chain of reasoning

Everything in this page follows from one fact: in the short run, some inputs are fixed.

Capital is fixed→Diminishing MP→Production function gets flatter→ TC gets steeper→MC rises→MC cuts AVC and ATC at their lowest points→U-shaped ATC

Mirror images

On the graphs, MP has the same color as MC, and AP the same color as AVC. That is because MC = w / MP and AVC = w / AP.

When MP is at its highest, MC is at its lowest. When AP is at its highest, AVC is at its lowest.

Fixed costs and the next unit

A higher rent moves AFC and ATC, but not AVC or MC (try it in Part 6). Fixed costs do not affect the cost of the next unit. Keep this in mind for Topic 7.
